Can you automate ecommerce customer support entirely? Most of it. Here is where the line sits, and how to build it

By Babar Lal  ·  15 October 2026  ·  11 min read

It is 11pm and the founder of a clothing store is still in the inbox. Where is my order. Can I change my size. My discount code did not work. Do you ship to Canada. Where is my order, again.

Buried under them is one message that matters. A jacket arrived torn, and the customer sent a photo two days ago. Nobody has replied yet, because the other forty messages came first.

Most of those forty could have been answered without anyone touching them. The torn jacket is the one that needed a person.

The short answer

You cannot automate all of it, and you should not try. You can automate most of the routine questions, so a person only sees the damaged parcels, the angry customers and anything outside your policy.

Every store is different, so count your own. Gorgias, a helpdesk vendor, says order status questions are 18% of requests on average in its data. It does not say how big the data set is or when it was collected. The same handful of types tend to fill the inbox.

The three layers that do the work

Layer one is self-serve. A tracking page with real delivery dates, a returns portal and a clear FAQ answer many questions before they are asked. Our posts on where-is-my-order automation and returns automation cover these two in depth.

Layer two is an AI agent with access to your store. It answers in chat, email, WhatsApp or text. It reads the order before it replies, so it can say "your parcel was scanned in Memphis this morning" instead of "check your email". It can also do a few safe things, like changing the size on an order that has not shipped.

Layer three is a person. Everything the agent is unsure about, or is not allowed to do, lands in one queue with a short summary, the order and the conversation attached. Nobody has to ask the customer to repeat themselves.

How to set it up, step by step

  1. Count last month's tickets by type. Start with the biggest type the table above marks as yes.
  2. Write down your policies so a machine could follow them. Return window, shipping times, what counts as damaged, and who pays.
  3. Connect the agent to your orders, tracking, catalog and stock. Read access first.
  4. Decide what it may change. A good first list is: send tracking, start a return inside policy, and edit or cancel an order that has not shipped.
  5. Set the hand-off rules. Damage, refunds outside policy, chargebacks, angry messages and anything it is unsure about go to a person, with a summary.
  6. Say it is an assistant, and make "talk to a person" easy to find.
  7. Read a sample of conversations every week for the first month. Fix wrong answers in the policy text, not by patching the bot.

The rules

This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

You are responsible for what your bot says. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, a chatbot gave a customer the wrong refund policy. The tribunal said "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot," and ordered the airline to pay. Keep your agent to your written policy, and keep that policy up to date.

Shipping delays have rules in the US. The FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule says you need a reasonable basis for the shipping time you state, or 30 days if you state none. If you cannot ship on time, you must ask the customer to agree to the delay and tell them they can cancel for a full refund. For most payment methods, the refund is due within seven working days of the cancellation. If your agent answers "where is my order", it should follow those rules when an order is late.

Customers want a way out. In a Gartner survey of 5,728 customers in December 2023, 64% said they would prefer companies did not use AI for service. Their top worry was that it would get harder to reach a person. An agent that answers fast and hands off easily is the answer to that worry. One that traps people proves it right.

When it backfires

No way to reach a person. A bot that loops "I can help with that" drives people to chargebacks and one-star reviews. Put the hand-off in every conversation.

A bot with no order access. If it can only say "check your email", it is a slower FAQ page. Connect it to orders and tracking, or do not launch it.

Making up policy. If the policy is not written down, the agent will fill the gap with something plausible. Moffatt v. Air Canada shows who pays for that.

Refunds with no limits. Let the agent start returns inside policy. Keep refunds outside policy, and anything above a set value, with a person.

Ignoring late orders. An agent that repeats a tracking link while an order is weeks late is not following the FTC's delay rules. Late orders should trigger a delay notice and the option to cancel.

Questions people ask

Can I automate my store's customer support entirely? Not entirely, and you should not try. Order status, returns inside policy, simple order changes and product questions can be answered or done with no person involved. Damaged items, refunds outside policy, chargebacks and angry customers still need a person. The goal is that a person only sees those.

What should I automate first? Where is my order. It is usually the biggest single type of ticket, and the answer already sits in your store and the carrier's tracking. Gorgias, a helpdesk vendor, says order status questions make up 18% of requests on average in its data.

What does an AI support agent need to work well? Read access to your orders and tracking, your written policies, and your product catalog. Without order access it can only say check your email. It also needs clear limits on what it may change, and a fast way to pass a conversation to a person with the order attached.

Am I responsible for what my chatbot tells customers? Assume you are. In Moffatt v. Air Canada in 2024, a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible for a wrong answer its chatbot gave, saying it makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot. Keep the bot to your written policies. This is not legal advice.

How much does ecommerce support automation cost? Shopify Inbox is free. In October 2026, Tidio listed its Lyro AI from $32.50 a month for 50 AI conversations, Gorgias charged about $0.90 to $1 per conversation its AI resolved, and Intercom listed Fin from $0.99 per outcome. A custom build has a setup fee and a monthly fee.

Will customers hate talking to a bot? Some will, if it blocks them. In a Gartner survey of 5,728 customers in December 2023, 64% said they would prefer companies did not use AI for service, and the top worry was that it would be harder to reach a person. Say it is an assistant and make the way to a person obvious.
