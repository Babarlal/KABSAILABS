Lead qualification and routing: stop chasing time wasters while good leads wait

By Babar Lal  ·  6 October 2026  ·  9 min read

It is 9am on a Tuesday at a real estate team. An agent opens the lead queue and starts calling. The first lead is just browsing and might buy in two years. The second has not spoken to a lender. The third wants a town the team does not cover. By 10:30 she has spent the morning on three people who were never going to buy.

The fourth lead in the queue came in at 8:52. She is pre-approved and wants to see a house on Saturday. When the agent finally calls at 11, she has already booked a showing with someone else.

The short answer

Ask two or three questions the moment a lead arrives. Then send it to the right person, with a time limit.

Simple ways to qualify a lead

The best-known checklist is BANT. It stands for budget, authority, need and timing. It comes from B2B sales.

For a service business, I use a plainer version with three questions. Can we help with this, in this area? Can they go ahead, with the budget or approval to do it? And when do they want it done?

For a real estate team, that becomes area, price range, financing and timing.

The answers sort leads into three groups. Ready now goes to a person straight away. Later goes into a follow-up sequence for leads who are not ready yet. Not a fit gets a polite reply.

What the data says about slow or misrouted leads

I covered the research on response times in our post on speed to lead. Two newer points matter for routing.

The first is about who wins the client. Zillow's 2025 Consumer Housing Trends Report for Agents found that 47% of buyers and 59% of sellers hired the first agent they spoke with. Zillow sells leads and tools to agents, so treat this as a vendor report. The agent who reaches the lead first often keeps them.

The second is about how often routing goes wrong. LeanData, which sells lead routing software, says one in four leads is routed incorrectly. Its April 2026 article does not say where that figure comes from, so treat it as a claim. I found no independent research on misrouting I could open.

How it works, step by step

  1. A lead comes in from a form, a portal, a chat, a text or a call.
  2. Within seconds, it gets two or three questions by text, chat or on the form itself. Our posts on AI website chat and WhatsApp and SMS support cover how those conversations work.
  3. The answers become a tag: ready now, later or not a fit.
  4. The lead is routed by fit. That means area, service, budget band, or the language the lead asked for.
  5. A time limit starts. If nobody claims the lead in five minutes, it moves to the next person who fits.
  6. Every answer, owner and touch is logged in your CRM.

For leads who are ready, the next step is often a booking. Our post on automatic appointment booking covers that part.

Routing rules that work, and ones that fail

Round robin hands leads out in turn. It feels fair. On its own, it ignores whether the agent covers that area, and whether they are even working today.

Territory routing sends leads by area or ZIP code. It works when areas are clear.

Specialism routing sends leads by type, such as rentals or luxury homes, or repairs or installs.

First to claim sends a lead to everyone and lets the fastest person take it. When nobody is watching, the lead just sits there.

The five-minute fallback is the safety net under all of these. If the first person does not respond, the lead moves on.

The setup I trust most: check fit first. Share leads fairly among the people who fit. Skip anyone who is off. Then add the fallback.

Fairness and honesty

This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Never pretend a bot is a person. If an AI asks the questions, it should say it is the team's assistant.

Route on what the lead needs, never on who they are. For real estate, HUD's overview of the Fair Housing Act says the Act protects people from discrimination when renting or buying a home. It lists race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status and disability. So your questions should be about the home, not the person. Do not ask about children, faith or where someone is from. Do not suggest or avoid neighborhoods based on who someone is. Route by language only when the lead asked for that language.

Outside housing, the safe rule is the same: qualify on need, budget and timing.

When it backfires

Questions that scare good leads off. Ten questions, or asking for income on the first message, makes people leave. Ask two or three, and say why.

Scoring nobody reviews. A score set up last year may now reward the wrong things. Compare tags with closed deals once a month.

Routing to someone on holiday. The lead waits all week. Mark people as away, and keep the five-minute fallback switched on.

A cold "not a fit" with no reply. Always reply politely, and put "later" leads into follow-up.

Questions people ask

What is lead qualification? It means finding out quickly whether a new enquiry is a good fit and ready to go ahead. For most service businesses, that comes down to a few plain questions: can we help, can they go ahead, and when. The answers decide who follows up and how fast.

How do you qualify a lead quickly? Ask two or three questions as soon as the lead arrives, by text, chat or on the form. For a home buyer, that might be area, price range and timing. For a service firm, it might be the job, the budget and the date. Tag the lead as ready now, later or not a fit.

What is lead routing? Lead routing is the set of rules that decides which person gets each new lead. Good rules send the lead to someone who covers that area or service and is working today. They also move the lead on if nobody picks it up within a few minutes.

What is the best way to route leads to agents? Check fit first, then share leads fairly among the people who fit. Skip anyone who is off today. Add a fallback, so a lead nobody claims within five minutes goes to the next person. Log every step in your CRM.

How do real estate teams qualify leads without breaking Fair Housing rules? Ask about the home, not the person. Area, price range, financing and timing are fair questions. The Fair Housing Act protects people based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status and disability, so never ask about or route on those. This is not legal advice.

Do small businesses need lead scoring software? Usually not at first. Two or three questions and a simple tag do most of the work. Points-based scoring helps once you have hundreds of leads a month and enough closed deals to check the scores against.
