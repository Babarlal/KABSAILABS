Getting paid without chasing people: automatic invoice reminders and failed payment recovery

By Babar Lal  ·  6 October 2026  ·  9 min read

There is a $2,400 invoice in the system. It is 47 days overdue. The owner sent one email at day 12 and heard nothing.

Now it feels awkward to ask again. The customer is nice, and there may be more work from them. So the invoice just sits there. Payroll is on Friday, and cash is tight.

The short answer

Let a schedule send the reminders, put a pay-now link in every one, and retry failed cards automatically. A person steps in only when someone replies, disputes the invoice or stays silent for weeks.

How common late payment is

The best open source I found is the Federal Reserve. Its 2025 Report on Employer Firms was published in March 2025. It is based on a survey of 7,653 small employer firms, run from September to November 2024.

51% of those firms said uneven cash flow was a financial challenge. The report says that includes collecting on receivables. Only rising costs were more common, at 75%.

One caution. The survey does not give a separate figure for late-paying customers. I could not find an open US government figure for how many invoices are paid late. Trade credit insurers publish surveys on this, but I could not open the figures, so I am not quoting them.

Why invoices go unpaid

An unclear invoice. No invoice number, no due date, or a description the customer's bookkeeper cannot match to anything.

The wrong contact. The invoice went to the person who hired you. The person who pays bills never saw it.

No easy way to pay. "Send a check" or bank details in a PDF means effort. A pay-now link means two clicks.

It was simply forgotten. This is the most common one, and the easiest to fix.

Disputed work. The customer is unhappy and says nothing. Reminders will not fix that. A conversation will.

Expired or declined cards. For subscriptions and saved cards, the payment fails and nobody notices until the next month.

A reminder schedule that works

  1. Three days before the due date. A friendly heads-up with the amount, the date and a pay-now link.
  2. On the due date. Short and plain: it is due today, here is the link.
  3. Three days late. A polite nudge that asks if the invoice reached the right person.
  4. Ten days late. Firmer. Give the amount, how late it is and how to pay.
  5. Around 21 days late, a person calls. Automatic emails have done their job by now.
  6. After 30 days, you decide. That might be a payment plan, a late fee if your terms allow it, or pausing work.

Reminders stop the moment the invoice is paid, disputed or answered. A reply goes to a person, not back into the queue. The same stop rules are in our post on following up leads who went quiet.

Failed card payments

If customers pay by saved card or subscription, some payments will fail. Most failures are temporary. The card hit its limit, or the bank was cautious.

Retry automatically. Stripe's Smart Retries pick the times to try again. Its recommended default is 8 tries within 2 weeks. Some declines cannot be retried, such as a lost or stolen card. Those need a new card.

Let the card update itself. Stripe says automatic card updates are widely supported in the US, for most American Express, Visa, Mastercard and Discover cards. When a bank issues a new card, the saved details can update without the customer doing anything.

Ask for a new card when you must. Stripe can email customers when a payment fails or a card is about to expire, with a link to update it. Subscription software companies live on this. Our SaaS automation page covers the rest of that billing flow.

The rules

This is a plain summary, not legal advice. Check the rules where you and your customers are.

Chasing your own invoices is different from debt collection by a third party. The federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is enforced by the FTC and the CFPB. It mainly covers third-party collectors, and only debts for personal, family or household purposes. The CFPB's rule, Regulation F, defines a debt collector as someone collecting debts owed to another. It leaves out a creditor's own staff collecting in the creditor's name. Some states have their own rules that can reach businesses collecting their own debts.

Late fees go in the contract first. If your terms did not say it, do not add it to a reminder.

Texting reminders needs consent and STOP handling. Your number also needs carrier registration. Our post on missed call text back and the US texting rules covers both.

When it backfires

Reminders on paid or disputed invoices. A customer who paid yesterday gets a "your invoice is overdue" email today. Sync payments before every send, and pause anything in dispute.

A robotic tone with good customers. A customer of ten years gets the same firm reminder as a stranger. Soften the wording for regulars, or have a person send the later ones.

Late fees that are not in the terms. The reminder threatens a fee nobody agreed to. Now you have an argument instead of a payment.

Reminders to the wrong contact. Five polite emails to a project manager who cannot pay anything. Check the billing contact when you send the first invoice.

Questions people ask

How do I automate invoice reminders? Start in your accounting software. QuickBooks Online can send up to three automatic reminders, each up to 90 days before or after the due date. Stripe Invoicing can send reminders before, on or after the due date. Put a pay-now link in every reminder, and switch reminders off for any invoice that is disputed.

What should a payment reminder email say? Keep it short. Give the invoice number, the amount, the due date and a link to pay. Early reminders can assume it slipped someone's mind. Later ones can be firmer and say what happens next. Always give a way to reply if something is wrong.

How many payment reminders should I send? I would start with one before the due date, one on the day, and two or three after it. After about three weeks a person should call. More automatic emails after that rarely help and can annoy a good customer who has a real problem.

What is dunning? Dunning is the process of chasing overdue payments. For invoices, it means a series of reminders. For subscriptions and saved cards, it also means retrying failed card payments and asking the customer for a new card when the old one stops working.

Can I charge a late fee on an overdue invoice? Only if the customer agreed to it first, in your contract or terms, and only within what your state allows. Adding a fee nobody agreed to usually starts an argument and can break the rules. This is not legal advice.

Do debt collection laws apply when I chase my own invoices? The federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act mainly covers third-party collectors chasing consumer debts. Your own staff collecting in your business's name are usually outside that definition. Some states have their own rules, and honest dealing still matters. This is not legal advice.
