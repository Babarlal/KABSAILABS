Lead follow-up: how to chase the leads who went quiet, without nagging or forgetting

By Babar Lal  ·  6 October 2026  ·  9 min read

On Monday, a plumbing company emails a homeowner a $4,200 quote for a new water heater and some repiping. She opens it. She has one question about why the repiping costs extra. She means to ask, but the week gets busy. Nobody from the company follows up.

Three weeks later, she books another company. That company texted her twice and answered her question on the second text. The first company never found out why it lost the job.

The short answer

Follow up five or six times over about two weeks, on more than one channel. Then drop to one useful message a month.

How many times should you follow up?

You have probably seen the claim that 80% of sales need five follow-ups. It is everywhere. I could not find the original study behind it. One 2026 write-up by Stylograph, a vendor, says it traces back to a poll of fewer than 40 people from 1942, and that no copy survives. I could not check that either. So I treat the 80% figure as folklore, not fact.

The best openable data I found is old and comes from a vendor. In a 2015 article, InsideSales said sales reps make only 1.3 call attempts on a new lead before giving up. It said reps can reach up to 90% of leads if they make at least six attempts. The data behind it goes back to a lead response study from 2007. So the exact numbers are dated, and they are about phone calls, not texts.

The direction is still useful. One attempt is far too few. Five or six, spread over a couple of weeks and across channels, is a sensible default. Then measure your own replies and adjust.

Following up only works if the first reply was fast. Our post on speed to lead covers that part.

Why leads go quiet

Silence is not a no. Leads usually go quiet for one of four ordinary reasons.

The timing was wrong. They asked in spring for a job they want in autumn.

They are still comparing. They have three quotes and have not sat down to read them.

The quote confused them. One line did not make sense, and asking felt awkward.

Life got in the way. A sick child, a work deadline, a holiday.

None of these is a reason to stop. Each one is a reason to send a different message.

A follow-up sequence that works

  1. Day 0. Send the quote, then a short text: "I have just emailed your quote. Any questions, reply here."
  2. Day 2. Text: "Did the quote make sense? Happy to walk you through any line."
  3. Day 4. Call. If there is no answer, leave a 20-second voicemail with your name and one reason to call back.
  4. Day 7. Email something useful. That might be photos of a similar job, payment options, or answers to common questions.
  5. Day 14. Close the loop: "Should I keep this open, or close it for now? Either is fine."
  6. After that, one useful email a month until they ask you to stop. Think seasonal tips or open slots, not discount blasts.

Every step stops the moment a person replies. A reply goes to a human, fast. If they are ready, the next step is a booking, as in our post on automatic appointment booking.

Leads you tagged as "later" during lead qualification and routing belong in the monthly nurture, not the two-week sequence.

The rules

This is a plain summary, not legal advice. Check your setup with your provider or a lawyer.

Texting needs consent and STOP handling. A follow-up about a quote someone asked for is a conversation, which the CTIA Messaging Principles treat more lightly than marketing. A monthly promotion is marketing and needs clearer consent. The FCC rule at 47 CFR 64.1200 treats replies like stop, quit, cancel and unsubscribe as a request to stop. Carrier registration for your number is covered in our post on missed call text back and the US texting rules.

Calling has hours. The same FCC rule says telephone solicitations to homes may not be made before 8am or after 9pm, in the time zone of the person you are calling. It also bars calls to numbers on the national Do Not Call registry. A call back about a quote someone requested is different from cold selling, but keeping to those hours is good practice anyway.

Email has CAN-SPAM. The FTC's compliance guide says commercial emails must not use misleading headers or subject lines. They must include your physical address and a clear way to opt out. Opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days. The law covers business-to-business email too. Treat your monthly nurture emails as marketing email.

If your customers prefer WhatsApp, the same respect applies. Our post on WhatsApp and SMS customer support covers Meta's opt-in rules.

When follow-up backfires

Too many messages. Daily texts for a week feel like pressure. People reply STOP, and then you cannot reach them at all.

The same message to everyone. A buyer who wants a viewing and a seller who wants a valuation need different words. Write a sequence for each main type of lead.

Follow-ups after they booked or said no. This happens when a booking made by phone never reaches the sequence. Make every booking and every "no" stop the automation.

Sequences nobody updates. Last year's prices or a holiday offer that ended in March make you look careless. Read your sequence once a quarter.

Questions people ask

How many times should you follow up with a lead? There is no proven magic number. I use five or six touches over about two weeks, mixed across text, email and a call, then one useful email a month. Stop the moment the person replies, books or says no.

How soon should you follow up after sending a quote? Send a short text the same day to say the quote is in their inbox. Then follow up about two days later and ask if they have questions. A quote can stall on one unanswered question.

Is it true that 80% of sales need five follow-ups? That figure is widely repeated, but I could not find the original study behind it. One 2026 write-up traces it to a small poll from the 1940s that no longer exists. Treat it as folklore, and measure your own numbers instead.

What should a follow-up message say? Keep it short and give a reason to reply. Ask if they have questions about the quote, share something useful, or offer a time. Never send the same message twice, and never just say you are checking in.

When should you stop following up? Stop at once if they book, say no, reply STOP or unsubscribe. Pause if they say not until later, and set a date to try again. After the main sequence ends, drop to one useful email a month until they ask you to stop.

Can I text leads to follow up? Yes, if they agreed to texts from you, for example when they filled in your form. Honor STOP and similar replies straight away. Your number also needs carrier registration. This is not legal advice.
